    READING MOVEMENT, BREATH & INTENTION

    The joins matter.

    Transitions

    In his later account, Tony Swanson describes coordination between shoulders and hips, elbows and knees, wrists and ankles. He distinguishes the first 50 movements from the more dynamic joins later in the form. Read the interview →

    Editorial study cue: compare the end of one lesson with the beginning of the next. Separately taught passages can conceal the linking movement.

    Breath

    Gibbon recommends natural breathing for beginners. Once the movements are familiar, his manual pairs inhalation with odd numbers and exhalation with even numbers, without forcing the breath. This is an attributed teaching convention, not a rule inferred from Chee Soo’s photographs. Manual, pp. 16–17 →

    Intention

    The animal and landscape names can focus attention, but they are not anatomical instructions. Editorial prompts in this explorer ask you to look closely at timing, direction and support. They are our reading aids, separate from a teacher’s explanation of qi or an application.

    Application notes identify their teacher. Where the evidence is missing, that limit stays visible.

    HISTORY & PROVENANCE

    What can be dated?
    What is testimony?

    1. 1976

      The published manual

      Seahorse’s bibliography dates Chee Soo’s first English edition to 1976, published by Gordon and Cremonesi. This is a publication anchor; it does not date every photographed exposure. Publisher bibliography →

    2. 1984 / 2003

      Later editions

      Aquarian Press issued the 1984 edition. Seahorse’s 2003 reprint is described by the publisher as retaining that edition’s photographs, text and page numbering. Edition record →

    3. 1992

      Chee Soo’s own account

      An edited interview with John R. Jones describes a 140-movement main form and distinguishes it from Flying Hands. It also records Chee Soo’s account of learning from Chan Lee. The surviving PDF is a 2005 edited reprint. Read the interview →

    4. Later teaching

      A living, varied practice

      Gibbon, Wood, Popplewell, Swanson and Ewers provide later records. Ewers explicitly recalls changes in Double Whip hand shapes and disputes some current methods. Those accounts remain attributed; they are not merged into one reconstructed pose. Read the comparison →

    Early lineage claims and conflicting counts

    Stories of an ancient Lee family transmission and Chee Soo’s meeting with Chan Lee belong to the tradition’s accounts of itself. Independent corroboration of the earliest genealogy has not been established here. Carl Pearson discusses the documentary gaps.

    An archived TAO page gives 142 movements in 41 sequences. Its date and the reason for that difference are unresolved. This explorer follows the explicitly attributed 140-movement index, corroborated by Chee Soo’s edited interview and later teaching records.

    The last video combines movements 138–140 under gathering and finishing. Wood lists those as two sets. We retain both names and their shared range rather than invent a numerical boundary.
